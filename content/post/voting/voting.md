## The Question of Civic Life 
I have never voted. I probably never will, unless I become a single issue voter for whatever keeps me employed. The movements of the political machine seem above me in a way I cannot substantially effect. To die for a cause means naught in a society and culture which has de-signified death, and so even release from my mortal coil will be integrated into the machine, not even making a *dent* in its operations. The machine does not need my consent. It fucks me and you all the same. It does not even need my opposition. It metabolizes all of it; the vote, protest, apathy, martyrdom and more. So what is left and what is there to do? This article entry is not an argument for voting, nor is it an argument for abstention. It is an attempt to reflect on what civic life means in the New Atlantis, and what it could *possibly* mean when the national political apparatus has revealed itself inept in its capability to reflect and produce a social substance- all things a functioning state ought to do.

## Elite Democracy and the Parapolitical

Now, the question of how power *actually* operates in the United States can’t be answered by a consultation with civics textbooks. The formal architecture of the Constitution, such as the checks and balances, separation of powers, etc., describes a system which has not existed practically in a long time, differing on how long depending on who you ask. Beneath the outward appearance, beneath the visible machinery of so called “democratic” governance, lies the convergence of corporate wealth, military apparatus, and political administration/managerialism which C. Wright Mills identified in 1956 as the “power elite”, and which subsequent scholars have refined into a theory of the dual state and parapolitics. This section is intended to examine the structure, and so does not begin from any premise approaching the idea that American democracy has been corrupted by external forces, or that it has simply failed to live up to its ideals, although analyses beginning with such premises may be acceptable. Rather, it begins with the recognition that the ideals themselves act as a legitimating mythology which obscures the actual distribution of power, while performing the work of securing consent. The ensuing perspectives offer one a vocabulary for describing the events of the highly contemporary period in America. 

### Classic Elite Theory
Classical elite theory emerged as a direct challenge to the Marxist and liberal assumptions dominating the political landscape of the time. The founders, Vilfredo Pareto (known most famously for the Pareto Principle), Gaetano Mosca, and the like, argued that every society, regardless of formal political arrangement, is governed by organized minorities. Under this view, democracy changes nothing but the terms under which elites rule, through various obfuscation and mediatory mechanisms. Pareto’s concept of the circulation of elites described history as a continuous process wherein one ruling group replaces another, without altering the underlying order of minority rule. Ultimately, Pareto observed that elites achieve their aims most effectively when the masses remain ignorant of their manipulation. As the quote from Twain goes, “It's easier to fool people than to convince them that they have been fooled”. Ergo, the futility of challenge from below is not so much a moral judgement about the capacity of the average joe as much as it is a description of how elite institutional channels (media, education, political parties, etc.) work simultaneously to impose preferred ideologies as what Pierre Bourdieu would moniker *doxa*. Classical elite theory offers a framework for comprehension of why electoral politics so rarely produces fundamental change. Because the choice presented to voters is always a choice between factions of the same elite class, the structural conditions which determine policy outcomes remain untouched by the outcome of any particular election. 

### The Power Elite
The abstract insights of what can be called classical elite theory coalesced into a concrete analysis of American society with the publishing of C. Wright Mill’s *The Power Elite* in 1956. For mills, a *triumvirate* (intentional terminology here referencing the New Rome) of interlocking institutional orders via the corporate, political, and military, that become enlarged and centralized in the postwar era (although one can trace this development to the Civil War with a glance reading of *Yankee Leviathan* by Richard Bensel). The corporate rich then controlled the giant corporations that held the keys to economic decisions and decision making, while the political directorate had taken up many powers previously scattered and now entered into all aspects of social organization. Meanwhile, warlords presided over a military establishment that had become the largest and most expensive part of government. Mill’s erudite analysis is aided by the observation of the groups’ intermingling dynamics. Considering the military-industrial complex that Ike had warned about in his farewell address is one manifestation of the broader convergence of institutional power, Mills has been unfortunately vindicated. Only now his descriptions have intensified with corporations becoming larger, the military more expensive, and the triumvirate more thoroughly integrated into the networks of private wealth.

### Mechanisms of Elite Rule
The power elite do not rule through decree as one may recognize in previous eras. The divine right of kings was deposed, and so now the form of rule is through *mechanisms*, through institutional arrangements that translate economic power and social power into political outcomes. The most visible of these is campaign finance. In the 2024 election cycle, Elon Musk alone spent around 288 million dollars to support his preferred candidates. Bribery may call out to one with common sense, but the technicality by which the name alludes the action is found in the operation of a system in which the funding of political campaigns has become a prerequisite for political viability, and those with the most resources to deploy have the greatest influence over who can complete and what positions they can afford to take. The revolving door is another such mechanism. When members of Congress, senior bureaucrats,and military officers leave governmental service, they often move into lobbying forms or think tanks or corporate boards or consultation and the like- any given position where their insider knowledge and personal networks become valued commodities. In more extreme cases, lobbyists draft the bills themselves. Think tanks, foundations, media outlets, and elite universities (the Ivy’s and such) form what scholars have called “partisan policy networks”, or “elite networks”. These are institutional constellations shaping public discourse, producing policy proposals, and most importantly, cultivating personnel who will staff future administrations. It may be said that “the system” is not rigged in the sense that a single cabal controls every decision. It is a negotiation of forces and interests which mediates into the outcomes of governance, all while maintaining the appearance of a pretense to democratic pluralism.

### Parapolitics and the Dual/Deep State
“Deep state” is a term originating from the work of Peter Dale Scott, who adopted the term (the original term was deep politics) from an application of the Turkish government’s actions. It refers to the unelected and informal levers of control in the modern state. Every modern state has one. This can be anything from whatever the military-industrial complex has cooking up, to advertising, to lobbying, to parties within the state that have a level of extralegal independence, to lies perpetuated by the media, to corporate influence, to the projects of intelligence agencies (sometimes on the domestic population), and so forth and so on. The concept of “parapolitics”, also as developed by Peter Dale Scott, describes a political system in which the “accountability principle is diminished”, that is to say, a system where covert means are used to exercise power, where public deception hides the instrumentalization of public agencies or structures and parastructures in achieving extra-judicial ends. As a theory of “conspiracy” in the colloquial sense, the aforementioned imagery of a cabal of hooded individuals, this is foreign. Instead, this framework extrapolates on conspiracy as an analysis of a structural feature of all modern states: the existence of a “dual state” comprising a public state, governed by law and subject to some kind of democratic accountability, and a deep state, which emerges in false flag violence, and is organized by the military and intelligence apparatus, involving their link to organized crime. The deep state is a set of institutional practices and informal networks; operational protocols that allow security and intelligence to act with a degree of autonomy from the formal political process. There is what can be called a monist notion of the state originating from the liberal framework that dominates political science. This is the assumption that the state is a unified rational entity understood through its official institutions. However, in the dual state, the “security state” operates according to a different logic than its public alternative. When the public state faces a political crisis or an issue that threatens the interest of the security apparatus, the deep state may intervene. Whether through covert operations, disinformation, or outright and blatant violence, it does not matter. Whatever means are justified if the ends are the prevention of disestablishing the perceived order. Of course the JFK assassination is the paradigmatic example, but it is not the only one. The Gulf of Tonkin incident, the Iran-Contra affair, Watergate, 9/11 and so forth demonstrate the deep state’s capacity to shape policy outcomes through means that bypass the formal democratic process. And these are only the famous ones. Terrorism on the domestic populace deserves a few books alone. Parapolitics reveals that the question of governance is not answered only by elections, it is in actuality extended and answered in part by the operational autonomy of the institutions that are supposed to be subordinate to elected officials. 

While the theoretical frameworks above are useful, using them as *practical* frameworks help them achieve their full enlightening power. The case studies mentioned above are not isolated aberrations from the norm. They are when the deep state’s operations become visible, intersecting with public consciousness. In other words, they are when the official narrative and actual events and mechanisms of power become too disjointed to ignore. For instance, the JFK assassination demonstrates that a sovereign executive could be eliminated by actors within the national security apparatus, and that the subsequent investigation could produce an official narrative. The Warren Commission's laughable “Magic Bullet” theory acted as an effectual simulacrum designed to replace and alleviate an intolerable reality. That is, intolerable to the liberal monist state. The Gulf of Tonkin incident demonstrated that the deep state could manufacture a pretext for war, manipulating intelligence and public perception to secure congressional authorization for escalation in Vietnam. Watergate… and so on and so on. The question of power and actuality boils down to this: *cui bono*?

## Lesser Evilism
The national political apparatus operates frequently according to logics that bypass and undermine the formal mechanisms of democratic accountability. Given this reality, citizens are still urged to participate in it anyway. The excuse for this lies in the rationale of lesser evilism, a discursive psychological mechanism which acts to transform the established structural undemocracy into a stable, self-reproducing political order. Lesser evilism is employed as a management method to bridge the gap between perceived individual ideals (which often aren’t thought through and are a result of those same parapolitical conditionings) and the parapolitical reality. It is how public energy is channeled away from structural questions and toward more symbolic ones, in addition to metabolizing opposition.

There is an allure to it all. In fact, even on face value, the logic of lesser evilism presents itself as a simple quantitative proposition. When faced with two options, one of which is worse than the other, what rational actor wouldn’t choose the lesser evil? And so to reject it appears to be a rejection of rationality itself, or a retreat into purity politics, or a selfish self-indulgent refusal to engage with the world as it really is. Sometimes it may be, but this does not automatically make the position right! No, the allure of lesser evilism lies precisely in this appearance of a practical realism. It positions itself as the mature and responsible position, the position of those who understand that politics is a negative freedom, that it consists of preventing the worst from happening rather than positing any program or normativity and attaining it. The seed of truth by which this framing sources its power is that in any given election, the choice between two candidates may indeed have different consequences for different populations. One candidate may pursue policies that inflict “less harm” than the other. This does not serve as an endorsement of lesser evilism as much as it is the bare faced observation that the world distributes suffering unevenly. The problem is when this recognition of difference is used to foreclose a more fundamental question. The question we are herded to ask is “which candidate is less bad”, but the question we are discouraged from asking is “how did we come to be faced with such an appalling choice in the first place?” The second question is certainly the one that matters, and it is the one that lesser evilism is designed to suppress on the one hand, and the question which leads to the treatment of parapolitics in the former half of this article on the other. So we must ask and see this logical employment for what it partly is, as a discursive practice manufacturing consent for a system that would otherwise be exposed as illegitimate. The psychological and political effects extend past the voting booth. By framing politics as a binary choice between two options, lesser evilism helps direct public attention toward the spectacular race and away from the structural conditions that produced the candidates' racing. Voters are encouraged to follow polls, debate performances, and so forth- all of which are forms of political theater that have no bearing on the actual distribution of power. 

The interlock between lesser evilism and parapolitics is the key to understanding how the system reproduces itself. Parapolitics limits the menu of choices. Lesser evilism makes us fight over the menu. The two work in tandem, finding the parapolitical structure ensuring that the range of viable political options is narrow, and the logic of lesser evilism ensures that public energy is expended within that narrow range rather than directed at the structures determining the boundary in the first place. Accordingly, there is a stable equilibrium maintained through consent, or more accurately, the manufacturing of consent. The population consents to the terms of the debate through persuasion rather than coercion. It is *persuaded* that the terms of the debate are unbiased and given. The parapolitical structure is rendered invisible and the *evil* choice is hyper-visible. Civic life is centered around the act of voting once every 4 years in a rigged election. Do what you will with this knowledge. 

## Spatial Differentiation
The most consequential political question available to the average person is not *whether* to participate, but *where* and *in what context*. The national and the local are not the same political object at different scales, they are different kinds, governed by different logics and offering a different “return on investment”. The national can be conceived of as a mediated hyperreal. Every encounter with it passes through an apparatus, whether it is through the network broadcast, polling aggregate, party brand, or security classification. Input by the citizen is aggregated into a statistic, and this statistic is then used to produce the reality it claims to measure. There is a precise sense in which participation here is symbolic- in the sense that it produces meaning and legitimacy for the system rather than affects the world. The parapolitical then completes the picture by identifying that the decisions that matter the most, that is those concerning war and surveillance and the like, are made within networks not designed to be responsible to electoral input and are frequently designed to be insulated from it. 

The local sphere can be cognized as effective and real. When a zoning board approves a warehouse on your street, or when a county prosecutor declines to charge, such outcomes are produced by identifiable people in identifiable rooms on determinate dates that can be attended, contested, etc. The feedback loop is short enough to close here, and the scale is small enough that a single committed person can shift an outcome. If this is true, then a dozen can change a policy. The practical implication is a reallocation of attention that is closer to triage than it is to strategy. The national sphere absorbs unlimited psychic energy and returns legitimacy to the machine. The local sphere absorbs modest energy and returns material change plus recognition. You still have to pay the toll, but the remaining efficacy belongs to actual civic capacity in close proximity.
